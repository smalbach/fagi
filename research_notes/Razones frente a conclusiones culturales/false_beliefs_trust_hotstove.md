## (a) Why false "avoid" beliefs persist: the hot stove effect and sampling biases

### Takeaway
The claim is well established. Denrell & March (2001) and Denrell (2005, 2007) show formally that adaptive sampling produces a bias. Learners stop sampling options that did badly early on, so they never correct an underestimate of those options, while they keep sampling and do correct an overestimate. This one-sided error correction means false "avoid" beliefs persist even under rational or optimal learning policies. Denrell (arXiv 2024) generalizes the result to adaptive sampling policies in general.

### Cited Findings
- **Denrell, J. & March, J. G. (2001).** "Adaptation as Information Restriction: The Hot Stove Effect." *Organization Science* 12(5): 523–538. DOI 10.1287/orsc.12.5.523.10092. The paper shows that adaptive processes (learning, competitive selection) reproduce successful actions. This creates a bias against alternatives that initially seem worse than they are, so it can account for apparent risk aversion and resistance to change without assuming a risk-averse trait. Verified. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.12.5.523.10092); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v12y2001i5p523-538.html)
- **Denrell, J. (2005).** "Why Most People Disapprove of Me: Experience Sampling in Impression Formation." *Psychological Review* 112(4): 951–978. Impressions depend on which observations get sampled, not only on how they are integrated. People stop interacting with those who made a bad first impression, so negative impressions are not corrected. Verified. — [APA PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2005-13637-011); [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/DENWMP)
- **Denrell, J. (2007).** "Adaptive Learning and Risk Taking." *Psychological Review* 114(1): 177–187. A risk-neutral learner who samples poor alternatives less can come to prefer a sure thing over a symmetric risky option with the same expected value, *even when following an optimal learning policy*. Negatively skewed options can instead produce risk seeking. Verified. — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ752159)
- **Denrell, J. (2024, arXiv 2404.02591).** "Adaptive Sampling Policies Imply Biased Beliefs: A Generalization of the Hot Stove Effect." Single author (Warwick). A related CogSci 2020 paper with the same title exists. Title and author verified; abstract not retrieved. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02591); [CogSci 2020 PDF](https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0400/0400.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rats learning socially about foods from what demonstrators *ate* (only positive transmission) looks like a design feature that, together with individual learning, keeps social transmission away from pure "avoid" signals. Avoid signals are exactly the ones that cannot be corrected by the receiver's own experience. This is a hypothesis for the report; I found no source that states it.
- The guppy result is the animal version of a socially transmitted hot stove. The tradition persists because naive fish never explore the alternative.

## (b) How social influence amplifies or corrects the hot stove effect

### Takeaway
Social influence cuts both ways. In Denrell & Le Mens, social influence acts on *which options people sample*. This can synchronize beliefs and lock in collective illusions, because people sample popular options and the unpopular ones stay unsampled and underrated. Toyokawa & Gaissmaier (2022) show that conformist social learning can instead *rescue* groups from the hot stove effect: a weak minority influence adds exploration through negative feedback. Budiono & Gureckis (2024) tested experimentally whether observing others' choices lets people escape "learning traps."

### Cited Findings
- **Denrell, J. & Le Mens, G. (2007).** "Interdependent Sampling and Social Influence." *Psychological Review* 114(2): 398–422. Influence over *sampling behavior* alone is enough to produce social influence on attitudes learned from experience. Others' attitudes change only what one is exposed to; for example, people are exposed to activities their friends enjoy. Verified. — [APA PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2007-05396-007); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Interdependent-sampling-and-social-influence.-Denrell-Mens/e7f5603171f9e1e9862ec60936c954c7fe28b96a)
- **Denrell, J. & Le Mens, G. (2017).** "Information Sampling, Belief Synchronization, and Collective Illusions." *Management Science* 63(2): 528–547. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2354. The model assumes people are more likely to sample popular than unpopular alternatives. That alone produces belief synchronization (a strong majority for one alternative) and *persistent collective illusions*. Verified. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2354)
- **Toyokawa, W. & Gaissmaier, W. (2022).** "Conformist social learning leads to self-organised prevention against adverse bias in risky decision making." *eLife* 11: e75308. The study combined models with online experiments: 185 participants performed the task alone and 400 in groups of 2–8 (585 total). Conformist influence promoted favourable risk taking even though many individuals were biased towards adverse risk aversion (the hot stove effect). Strong positive feedback from copying the majority *could* produce bad informational cascades. However, the differential-equation model found a key role for extra exploration driven by *negative feedback*, which arises when a weak minority influence undermines the inherent bias. The authors call this "collective behavioural rescue." Group participation made information sampling less biased over time. Earlier preprint titles were "Conformist social learning inoculates against adverse risk aversion" (bioRxiv 2021.02.22.432286) and, on GitHub, "Conformity leading self-organised rescue…". Verified via search snippets; full text blocked. — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/75308); [PubMed 35535494](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35535494/); [GitHub](https://github.com/WataruToyokawa/ToyokawaGaissmaier2021); [phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2022-05-greater-comfort-riskier-behavior.html)
- **Budiono & Gureckis (2024).** "How does social learning affect stable false beliefs?" CogSci 2024 (Rotterdam); also presented at CCN 2024. The paper studies whether "learning traps" persist when individual and social learning are both present. It builds on prior evidence that people learn from others' *choices* without seeing outcomes, and that seeing exploratory choices by others can make people more exploratory, which could keep them out of traps. First names and the quantitative results were not retrieved (PDF blocked). — [Gureckis lab PDF](https://gureckislab.org/publications/budiono2024socialtrap-cogsci.pdf); [CCN 2024](https://2024.ccneuro.org/pdf/510_Paper_authored_DLT_CCN_Authored.pdf)
- Related network-epistemology result: **O'Connor, C. & Weatherall, J. O. (2018).** "Scientific polarization." *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 8: 855–875. In a Bala–Goyal network-epistemology model (agents test options and share results), stable polarization on a factual question emerges when agents *discount evidence from others whose beliefs differ from their own*. This happens even though true belief pays better. Verified. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-018-0213-9); [arXiv 1712.04561](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.04561)

### Inferences
- In Bala–Goyal type models, agents who believe an option is worse stop testing it, so they generate no evidence about it. That is the hot stove effect at network level. Correction must come from *other* agents who still sample the option. Social learning therefore helps when it keeps some agents exploring (Toyokawa's minority-induced exploration). It hurts when it lowers the diversity of sampling (Denrell & Le Mens 2017 popularity sampling; strong conformity cascades).
- For false *avoid* beliefs, the danger lies in social transmission of the avoid label *without* the underlying experience. Receivers inherit the avoidance, never sample, and the belief loses its only correction channel.

- The findings of Budiono & Gureckis (help vs. entrench) could not be read.

## (c) Epistemic vigilance, source trust and provenance: do they reduce false-belief persistence?

### Takeaway
Epistemic vigilance is a well-established theoretical framework (Sperber et al. 2010), and children's selective trust in accurate informants is robustly documented. Formal Bayesian models of source reliability exist (Merdes, von Sydow & Hahn 2021). I found **no population-level model that directly tests whether tagging beliefs with source and trust reduces the persistence of false "avoid" beliefs.** Existing trust-weighted models show that trust can also *produce* stable false beliefs, as in O'Connor & Weatherall's polarization. I found no cultural-evolution ABM that tracks per-belief genealogy or provenance (who originated each false belief, full transmission trees). The nearest analogues are epidemic-style "R0 of an infodemic" estimates and rumor source-detection ("patient zero") algorithms.

### Cited Findings
- **Sperber, D., Clément, F., Heintz, C., Mascaro, O., Mercier, H., Origgi, G. & Wilson, D. (2010).** "Epistemic Vigilance." *Mind & Language* 25(4): 359–393. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0017.2010.01394.x. Humans depend massively on communication, which exposes them to accidental or intentional misinformation. For communication to stay advantageous, humans have cognitive mechanisms of epistemic vigilance directed at both the source (competence, benevolence) and the content. Verified. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0017.2010.01394.x); [Sperber PDF](https://dan.sperber.fr/wp-content/uploads/EpistemicVigilance.pdf); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1331363/)
- **Koenig, M. A. & Harris, P. L. (2005).** "Preschoolers mistrust ignorant and inaccurate speakers." *Child Development*. Four-year-olds, but not 3-year-olds, predicted an informant's future accuracy and sought and endorsed information from the accurate one. Both ages preferred knowledgeable over ignorant speakers. Verified (title and journal; volume and pages not captured). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2005.00849.x)
- **Harris, P. L., Koenig, M. A., Corriveau, K. H. & Jaswal, V. K. (2018).** "Cognitive Foundations of Learning from Testimony." *Annual Review of Psychology* 69: 251–273. Verified. — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-122216-011710)
- **Merdes, C., von Sydow, M. & Hahn, U. (2021).** "Formal models of source reliability." *Synthese* 198 (Suppl 23): S5773–S5801. The paper compares Bayesian source-reliability models (Bovens & Hartmann; Olsson) conceptually and in simulations; the models differ subtly in assumptions and behavior. Verified. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-02595-2)
- **O'Connor & Weatherall (2018)**, see (b). When trust in others' evidence depends on how close their beliefs are, it *produces* stable false-belief factions. So trust tagging alone is not guaranteed to correct. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-018-0213-9)
- **Cinelli, M., Quattrociocchi, W., Galeazzi, A., et al. (2020).** "The COVID-19 social media infodemic." *Scientific Reports* 10: 16598. The authors fitted epidemic models to COVID-19 information spread on Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, Reddit and Gab and estimated a basic reproduction number R0 per platform. All R0 values were supercritical (>1). Information from reliable and questionable sources did *not* show different spreading patterns. This is an empirical "R0 of misinformation," but it is not a provenance-tracking ABM. Verified. — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-73510-5)
- **Kong, L.-W., Gallart, L., Grassick, A. G., Love, J. W., Nayak, A. & Hein, A. M. (2025).** "A brief natural history of misinformation." *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 22(233): 20250161. DOI 10.1098/rsif.2025.0161. A review of socially transmitted misinformation across biological systems, from bacteria to birds to humans, which argues that producing and transmitting misinformation is probably inevitable. Verified. — [Royal Society](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/22/233/20250161/364004/A-brief-natural-history-of-misinformation)
- Rumor source detection ("patient zero" inference in independent-cascade and SIR models) is a mature subfield of network science. It infers the *origin* of a spreading item from cascade observations. It is not about agents that carry provenance tags. — [Survey, arXiv 2101.00753](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.00753); [arXiv 2205.12125](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12125)
- Vosoughi, S., Roy, D. & Aral, S. (2018), "The spread of true and false news online," *Science* 359: 1146–1151, reconstructed retweet cascade trees and found that false news spread farther, faster, deeper and more broadly than true news. **[UNVERIFIED this session]**
- Mercier, H. (2020), *Not Born Yesterday: The Science of Who We Trust and What We Believe* (Princeton UP), argues that people are more vigilant than gullible. **[UNVERIFIED this session]**

### Inferences
- Epistemic vigilance evaluates the source and the *plausibility* of content. An "avoid X" message is cheap to accept and costly to test, so vigilance toward content gives little protection. Only source-reliability tracking can help, and source reliability itself has to be assessed by testing claims. The receiver of an avoid belief never tests it, so reliability estimates for sources that spread avoid-beliefs are never updated. This is a hot stove effect on *source* trust. It is my own synthesis; I found no paper that states it.
- An ABM that tags each belief with its origin and transmission chain (a per-belief phylogeny) and measures whether trust-weighting shortens the lifetime of false avoid beliefs appears to be an **open gap**. Cultural-evolution work on transmission chains (e.g., Laland & Williams) and misinformation R0 estimates exist, but they are aggregate. They do not track per-belief genealogy.

### Gaps
- No source found for an ABM that tracks provenance or genealogy of individual false beliefs in cultural evolution. Searches for "provenance," "transmission tree," "genealogy" and "patient zero" returned only source-detection algorithms and generic misinformation ABMs.
- No source found that tests experimentally or formally whether source tagging reduces the population-level persistence of false beliefs. Mercier's and Sperber's work is mostly theoretical or individual-level.

## (d) Optimal social vs. individual learning under environmental change

### Takeaway
Rogers (1988) showed that under environmental change, pure social learners do not raise mean population fitness at equilibrium (Rogers' paradox). The paradox is resolved by *selective* strategies. "Critical social learning" (Enquist, Eriksson & Ghirlanda 2007) copies first and falls back on individual learning if the result is unsatisfactory. The social learning strategies tournament (Rendell et al. 2010) found that near-exclusive social learning wins when demonstrators show their best behavior, and that the winner discounted information by its age. Kendal et al. (2018) review the resulting "what, when, whom to copy" framework.

### Cited Findings
- **Rogers, A. R. (1988).** "Does Biology Constrain Culture?" *American Anthropologist* 90(4): 819–831. A model of social learning in a variable environment: at evolutionary equilibrium, social learning does not raise the population's mean fitness (Rogers' paradox). Verified. — [Wiley](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/aa.1988.90.4.02a00030); [PDF](https://www.cognitionandculture.net/wp-content/uploads/Rogers-AA-90-819.pdf)

This strategy always beats pure social learning and usually beats pure individual learning. Verified (full author list from memory **[partly UNVERIFIED]**). — [Cell/TICS](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/abstract/S1364-6613(18)30094-9); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1334735/social-learning-strategies-bridge-building-between-fields)

### Inferences
- The fix for Rogers' paradox is critical social learning: copy, then test and revise if unsatisfactory. That fix **does not work for avoid beliefs**. A copied "avoid X" produces no outcome that could turn out unsatisfactory, because X is never tried. So the standard resolution leaves exactly the hot stove class of false beliefs uncorrected, unless some agents keep exploring (Toyokawa) or information is discounted by age (discountmachine).
- The tournament winner's recency discounting is a crude form of provenance or time tagging. It suggests that *metadata about when and how information was acquired* is adaptive under volatility. That links (c) and (d).
- The tournament's key mechanism, "demonstrators filter information for copiers," requires demonstrators to *act on* their best behavior. Avoidance is the absence of an action, so for avoid beliefs the filter works only in the harmful direction: nobody demonstrates the avoided option's true payoff.

### Gaps
- Studies of learning *under volatility* that model avoidance-type beliefs specifically (asymmetric correction plus social transmission plus environmental change) were not found. This combination appears under-studied beyond Toyokawa & Gaissmaier (static environment) and the Laland & Williams guppy chains.
- Rendell et al. (2011) "Cognitive culture…" (*TICS*) and later SLS tournament follow-ups were not verified.
